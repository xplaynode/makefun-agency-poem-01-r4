## C01 — The Price She Said Aloud

For three years the app set her prices.
It read the weather, the ferry times,
and told her what the dried anchovies were worth
before she had lifted the basket lid.

This morning Pak Haji came as he always had,
twenty years, same blue shirt, same torn sandal,
and asked for two kilos for his wife's stall.

The phone buzzed with a number.
She looked at it. She looked at his hands,
the knuckles thick from gutting fish at five.

She put the phone face down on the scales.
"No," she said. "For you, this."
And named a price from her own mouth,
higher than the app's, lower than last week's,
because she knew his wife was ill
and knew he would never say so.

He laughed once, short.
"There you are," he said.
They argued then, properly,
over ten rupiah, over nothing,
both of them grinning at the sound of it.

## C02 — Seven Hundred Metres

For six years they drove him everywhere.
Son to the clinic, granddaughter to prayers,
neighbour with the grey pickup to the mosque.
He sat in back and watched his own street pass.

This morning he put on the brown shoes
no one had cleaned, because no one walked in them,
and left while the kettle was still loud.

Seven hundred metres to the corner
where his stall stood for forty years.
The new boy sells fried bananas there now,
oil too dark, bananas cut too thick.

His knees said stop at the warung awning.
His knees said stop again at the drain bridge.
He stopped. He breathed. He did not turn back.

At the stall he did not buy anything.
He stood where the wok used to be
and put his palm flat on the warm counter,
and the boy, not knowing why, moved aside.

## C03 — Let Go, He Said

He ran behind the bicycle for nine days,
one hand on the seat, on Jalan Kenanga,
past the laundry, past the sleeping cat,
shoes slapping, breath gone by the third house.

His son never pedalled harder.
He pedalled exactly as fast as the hand allowed,
checking, with every turn, that it was there.

On the tenth day they reached the mango tree
where the road dips and the stones show through.
His son said, without looking back,
"You can let go now."
"I'm not ready," he said, and it was true —
his hand had learned the weight of the boy
the way it once learned a trade.

"Then run out of breath," his son said.
So he let his fingers open, one by one,
not brave, just out of air,
and watched the small back go on without him,
wobbling, then straight, then gone around the bend.

## C04 — The Third Plant From the Fence

The garden app flagged it on Tuesday:
third plant from the fence, leaves curled,
yield forecast zero, remove and replace.
A red X sat on its photograph all week.

She had planted that row in March,
twelve bird's eye chilies, in soil she had
carried herself in the cracked blue bucket,
two trips, because one trip hurt her shoulder.

The third plant was weak. That was plain.
Its stem leaned like it was listening.
But on Thursday a bud had opened anyway,
white, no bigger than a fingernail paring.

She deleted the alert. Then the schedule.
Then she tied the stem to a chopstick
with the green string from a rice sack,
and watered it last, by hand, from a cup,
the way you water something
you have decided about yourself.

In August it gave her four chilies.
She put all four in one sambal
and ate it without telling anyone why it burned so well.

## C05 — The Road Past the Depot

For eleven years he drove the route they gave him.
Turn here. Stop there. Nineteen stops,
the list printed fresh every Monday,
his name spelled wrong on it every time.

He could have driven it sleeping.
Sometimes, past the depot at noon,
he thought that he had.

Today the list said left at the cement works,
the long way, past the new toll pillars.
Ahead was the old road through Kalibata lanes,
where the mango sellers wave without looking up,
where he courted his wife on a borrowed bike.

He sat at the junction with the indicator ticking.
Left was his job. Straight was twenty minutes
of a road he had not chosen in eleven years.

He turned off the indicator.
He drove straight, slowly,
past the sellers, past the repair shop
where his brother still owes him money,
and arrived twelve minutes late,
with his windows down and his shirt smelling of rain.

## C06 — He Came Back With Tea Money

For two years he sold through messages.
A cousin photographed the woven mats,
a nephew typed the prices, answered, argued.
He stayed in the back room rolling palm fibre
and never heard a buyer's voice.

Last week the messages got his best mat wrong —
called the river pattern by the town's name,
sold it cheap to a man who wanted wall colour.
He read the thread three times.

This morning he took the bus to the market himself,
six mats on his shoulder, smelling of river water,
and stood in the heat where he used to stand.

The buyer from the thread came at ten.
In person he was younger, and shy of the price.
They argued standing up, mat unrolled between them,
hands on the pattern, river under their fingers.

He got his price. Then he did not leave.
He bought two teas from the stall next door
and they drank them sitting on the unsold mats.

## C07 — One Cut, Made Late

The service had it booked for Thursday:
the old grapevine over the back fence,
trimmed, cleared, photographed, done.
She got the reminder on the plane home.

She had not lived in that house for nine years.
The vine had. Her mother had trained it
along three wires with blue plastic ties,
and every March someone in the family
was meant to cut it back, hard, before the sap rose.

Thursday was too late and too early —
the man would cut where the diagram said,
not where the vine had blackened in January,
not sparing the one thick spur her mother
had marked with nail polish, red, still there.

She landed at dusk with her bag still packed.
In the shed the shears were rusted at the hinge.
She worked them open with oil and patience,
stood on the crate, and made the cut herself —
one cut, uneven, a thumb's width too long,
sap on her wrist, in the last of the light.

## C08 — His Hand Under Her Back

The instructor was kind and young and sure.
"Lie her back, she'll float, they all float,"
she told the line of parents at the pool rail,
and the other children went limp as dolls.

His daughter looked at the water, then at him,
in his work shirt, shoes still on, at the rail.
She did not cry. She held the rail herself,
which is what she does instead of crying.

He took off his shoes. He did not ask.
He got in in his shorts past the shallow steps,
the water cold up to his ribs in one breath,
and the instructor stepped back without a word.

His hand went under her small back,
flat, shaking a little, hers to trust or not.
For a long minute she stayed stiff as a board.
Then her ears went under, her hair spread out,
and her weight came down into his palm —
all of it, given, not taken,
while he stood there holding up the whole sky.
